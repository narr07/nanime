## What's happening

The clock opens already showing the real time, standing still. Pressing
play starts the hands moving, at whatever speed is currently set: `×1`
plays a full day in a full day, so mostly what's visible moving is the
second hand, while `×4` sweeps the whole face round noticeably. Reverse
sends the hands backward from wherever they currently are, restart snaps
back to midnight, and the slider jumps to any point in the day directly.

## How it's built

The clock isn't several composables layered together, it's one
[`useAnimeTimeline`](/composables/use-anime-timeline) with a single
timing-only entry added to it: `timeline.add({ duration: 86400000 })` (24
hours in milliseconds), no target, no property to tween. A `Timeline`'s
own constructor always starts its `duration` at `0` and grows it from
whatever gets added to it, a `duration` passed in the options object
passed to `useAnimeTimeline` itself is silently ignored. Adding one entry
with nothing but a duration is the same trick `Timeline`'s own internal
`.call()` method uses to schedule a callback at a point in time without
animating anything, borrowed here to give the timeline a real 24-hour
length to scrub, play and reverse through.

Every visible change, the three hand angles, comes from reading
`self.currentTime` inside `onUpdate` and computing each hand's angle from
it in plain JavaScript. The hands themselves are not targets of any
`nanime` animation, they're just template bindings on a computed angle.

Play, pause, reverse and restart call `timeline.play()`,
`timeline.pause()`, `timeline.reverse()` and `timeline.restart()`
directly. `useAnimeTimeline`'s buffered proxy makes these, and `add` and
`seek`, safe to call immediately, even before the underlying instance
exists: they queue up and replay once it's created. Starting position and
speed work differently: `currentTime` and `speed` are plain properties on
the real instance, not buffered methods, so writing them before mount
would silently do nothing, there's no instance yet to receive the write.
The initial speed is passed as `playbackRate` in the timeline's own
options instead (read into `speed` at creation), and the initial position
is set with `timeline.seek(...)` rather than a direct
`timeline.currentTime = ...` assignment, for the same reason `add` is used
for duration instead of a `duration` option.

## Building it yourself

1. Call `useAnimeTimeline({ autoplay: false, loop: true, playbackRate:
   <starting speed>, onUpdate, onBegin, onPause })`.
2. Immediately call `timeline.add({ duration: 86400000 })`, a single
   no-op entry that gives the timeline a real 24-hour duration. A
   `duration` option in step 1 would be silently overwritten to `0` by
   the `Timeline` constructor itself, this is the actual way to set it.
3. In `onUpdate`, read `self.currentTime` into a ref and derive hour,
   minute and second angles from it with plain arithmetic.
4. Bind each clock hand's `rotate` from its angle in a computed style.
5. Set the starting position with `timeline.seek(Date.now() % 86400000)`,
   not a direct property assignment, the instance doesn't exist until
   after mount and only buffered methods survive that gap.
6. Wire Play/Pause/Reverse/Restart buttons to `timeline.play()`,
   `.pause()`, `.reverse()`, `.restart()`. All four are safe to call from
   the start.
7. For a speed control, change `timeline.speed` directly from a button's
   click handler or a slider's input handler, by then the instance
   already exists so a plain property write reaches it.
